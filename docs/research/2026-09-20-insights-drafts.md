### EN — Google's assistant is going to call your business. Who picks up?

**Slug:** `googles-assistant-will-call-you` · **Read:** 3 min
**Description:** Google now phones local businesses on a customer's behalf to ask about prices and availability. Voicemail is a lost job. Here is what the call sounds like, and how to be the business it recommends.

Since last November, a person in McAllen can type "dog groomer near me" into Google, tap a button, and Google's own assistant rings the businesses on the map to ask what they charge and when they can fit someone in. The customer gets a text with the answers. This summer Google widened it from stock checks at stores to home repair, beauty and pet care.

Read that again from your side of the counter. The first contact from a new customer may now be a machine calling your line on a weekday afternoon, asking three plain questions. It will not leave a voicemail and try again later. It will report "no answer" and move to the next name on the list.

## What the call sounds like

It says who it is. Then it asks whether you have the thing, what it costs, and when. It is polite, it is quick, and it is grading you. Google lets you switch these calls off from your Business Profile, which is the wrong answer in the same way that unplugging the phone is the wrong answer.

## Three things decide whether you get recommended

- **Somebody, or something, answers.** A ring-out is a zero. A full mailbox is a zero. "Please hold" for four minutes is, in practice, a zero.
- **The answer has numbers in it.** "It depends, bring it in" is a zero. A price range and a real window is a score. The assistant is collecting exactly two facts, and the business that has them ready wins.
- **Your Google profile is true.** The assistant calls the businesses Google already trusts, in the order Google already ranks them. Wrong hours or a missing service category and you were never on the list.

## The uncomfortable part

It is not only Google. Every phone in the Valley now carries an assistant that can make a call. Within a year, a meaningful share of first calls to a plumber, a clinic or a groomer will be one assistant talking to another. The business that answers with a voice that can quote, book, and switch to Spanish mid-sentence wins that exchange every time. The business that sends it to voicemail never finds out it was in the running.

## What we do about it

Sofía answers our line and our clients' lines the same way for a person and for an assistant: she says what she is, she quotes from a sheet the owner keeps current, and she books into the calendar. `[editorial choice]` We are teaching her to recognise an automated caller and log it as its own outcome, so on Monday you can see how many of last week's calls were an assistant shopping for someone, and how many of those you were able to answer with a price.

## Where to start

Call your own business number from your cell at 2:15 on a Tuesday. Count the rings. That is the test Google is running, and you can run it first.

### EN — Texas will not punish you for a breach if you did the basics. Here are the basics.

**Slug:** `texas-safe-harbor-basics` · **Read:** 3 min
**Description:** Since September 2025, a Texas business with fewer than 250 employees that keeps a written, working security program is shielded from punitive damages after a breach. For a shop under 20 people, the bar is lower than you think.

A year ago Texas passed a law most Valley owners have never heard of, and it is the rare one written in your favour. Since September 1, 2025, if your business has fewer than 250 employees, holds sensitive personal information (Social Security numbers, licence numbers, bank details, health records) and gets breached anyway, a court cannot add punitive damages on top, provided you had a documented security program running at the time.

It does not make you immune. You still owe actual damages, still answer to the Attorney General, still notify the people affected. What it removes is the part of a verdict designed to punish, which is the part that closes small businesses.

## The bar is sized to you

- **Under 20 employees:** a password policy and staff awareness training. Written down. Followed.
- **20 to 99:** a public checklist called CIS Controls, Implementation Group 1: multi-factor sign-in, backups, updates, and a list of who can get into what.
- **100 to 249:** a full framework of the kind a hospital or a bank uses.

Most Valley clinics, law offices, freight brokers and contractors sit in the first two tiers. So the difference between protected and exposed is a written policy, a training record, and proof both existed before the bad day.

## Why "written down" is the whole point

The law does not ask whether you were careful. It asks whether you can show a program was in place when the breach happened. A password rule everybody sort of follows is not a program. A one-page policy dated last March, a sign-in sheet from a 40-minute training, and a note of who reviewed it: that is a program. The paperwork is the protection.

## The Valley has already had its warnings

In February 2025 the City of Mission's network was attacked. A month later DHR Health, the region's largest hospital system, took its systems down after a cyber incident and patients were turned away from appointments. Neither was a giant corporation in another state. Both were down the road.

## Two things worth knowing this month

Texas now runs a Cyber Command out of San Antonio, and its small-business centre at UTSA gives smaller organisations free training, advising and assessments. And the federal HIPAA update that would make multi-factor sign-in and encryption mandatory for every clinic is still not final; the target date slipped to mid-2027. Do not wait for it. The Texas law already rewards you for doing those things now.

### EN — The Valley is not behind on AI. It is early.

**Slug:** `the-valley-is-early` · **Read:** 4 min
**Description:** Latino-owned businesses more than doubled their use of AI in a year, and nearly half already sell across a border. The Valley's habits look like disadvantages on paper and advantages in practice.

There is a story the Valley tells about itself, usually in a tone of apology: we are behind. Big-city firms get the technology first, and we get it later, secondhand, if at all.

The numbers do not support it. Stanford's State of Latino Entrepreneurship report, published this April, surveyed more than ten thousand business owners with at least one employee. Latino-owned firms adopted AI at the same rate as white-owned firms, and their use more than doubled in a single year. They were more likely to be weighing an acquisition. Nearly half operate internationally, and the ones that do report better margins than the firms that stay home.

That is not a region catching up. That is a region that was already doing the hard part.

## Three Valley habits that turn out to be AI habits

- **Two languages, every day.** An assistant that switches from English to Spanish mid-call is not a novelty here; it is the minimum. Businesses that already run bilingual are the ones AI serves best, because the difficult problem, knowing what your customers actually say, is already solved at the counter.
- **Business across a bridge.** Reynosa and Matamoros are part of the Valley economy, and the agreement that governs that trade is now reviewed every year instead of every six. Owners used to the rules shifting under them are exactly the owners who pick up new tools quickly, because they have never had the luxury of waiting for things to settle.
- **Owner-answered.** At a lot of Valley businesses the owner still picks up the phone. That is a strength and a ceiling. AI's first job here is not to replace anyone. It is to answer the call the owner could not, at 6:40 PM, in the language it arrives in, and write it down.

## What is actually behind

Capital and access. Latino-owned firms received under two percent of venture funding last year and are turned down for loans without a stated reason more often than anyone else. The free small-business AI workshops that OpenAI, Google and Meta ran across Texas over the past year went to Houston, Richardson, Frisco and San Antonio. Not one came to the Valley. Meanwhile UTRGV is graduating computer engineers into a McAllen tech job market that shrank over the past year, and they are queuing at hospital career fairs.

None of that is a talent problem or a willingness problem. It is a distribution problem. The tools arrive here last, and the training arrives never.

## What we are doing about it

We built our software here and we run our own shop on it, in both languages, because that is where the Valley's advantage sits. `[editorial choice]` If a free, bilingual AI session for Valley owners would be useful to you, tell us. If enough people say yes, we will run one in Harlingen this fall.

## Where to start

Do not start with a tool. Start with the one question your customers ask most often, in whichever language they ask it. If a machine could answer that correctly at 6:40 PM, what would that be worth to you this year? That number is your budget, and it is usually larger than the software.

### EN — Texas has an AI law now. Here is the paragraph that applies to you.

**Slug:** `the-texas-ai-law-paragraph` · **Read:** 3 min
**Description:** The state's AI act took effect January 1 and the Attorney General's complaint form opened this month. For most Valley businesses the obligation is short. For a clinic it is one sentence longer.

On January 1 the Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act took effect, and this month the Attorney General opened an online form where anyone can file an AI complaint. Law firms have spent the year writing about it. Here is the version for someone who runs a business rather than a practice group.

## What it forbids

Using AI on purpose to discriminate against a protected group, to manipulate a person into harming themselves or others, or to make unlawful deepfakes. If you use an assistant to answer the phone, draft quotes or sort leads, none of this is you. The law also says a lopsided outcome on its own is not a violation. Intent is.

## What it asks of you anyway

Only the Attorney General can enforce it, and before doing anything the office must send you a written notice and give you 60 days to fix the problem. Fixing it closes the matter. What the office can ask for in that window is documentation: what the system is for, what goes into it, what comes out, what its limits are.

So the practical obligation is a list. Which AI tools you use, from whom, for what. Keep it current. A vendor who cannot describe their own system on one page is the compliance gap, not you.

## The extra sentence for clinics and dental offices

If you provide health care, you must tell patients, clearly and in plain language, when they are dealing with an AI system, no later than the first time it is used in their care. A separate Texas law adds that AI used for diagnosis must be disclosed and that electronic records must be physically kept in the United States. A line on the intake form and a sentence from the front desk cover most of it.

Sofía tells every caller she is an assistant. She did before any statute asked, because we think a person is owed that whether or not a law says so.

## What "reasonable care" looks like at your size

The law presumes you acted reasonably, and following a recognised AI risk framework is a named defence. For a small business that means the list above, a sentence naming who is responsible for it, and a habit of checking that the tool still behaves the way it did when you bought it. Congress may yet pause state AI laws. The list is worth keeping regardless, because the next customer who asks what you do with their information will want the same page.
