Visual direction · BIS Platform

From Functional to Finished

The platform works. What it lacks is depth, hierarchy, and moments of brand — the three things that separate an internal tool from a product people pay monthly for. Everything below is buildable with CSS variables on top of the theming engine you already shipped.

01 · Diagnosis

Why it currently reads as "basic"

None of these are taste problems — each is a specific, fixable mechanic.

One flat elevation

Every surface is the same near-black card on a near-black page with a hairline border. With no elevation ladder, nothing recedes and nothing advances — the eye has no landing point.

No data ink

A CRM's beauty is its data. Right now numbers sit alone in cards: no sparklines, no deltas, no trend bars. Empty pixels where evidence of value should be.

Typography without a scale

One weight, two sizes, no display face. Page titles, card labels, and body copy all carry similar visual weight, so hierarchy is done by position alone.

Unused width

Voice, Calendar, and Setup put a single narrow column on a 1500px canvas. Whitespace is a tool; a dead right half is just absence.

Unbranded key moments

The login door, the booking page, empty states — the moments a client's customer sees are the least designed surfaces in the product. That's backwards.

Affordance gaps

Checkboxes without bulk actions, chevrons that need pixel-perfect clicks, no hover states on rows. Small frictions compound into "feels unfinished."

02 · Foundations

The kit: surfaces, type, and rhythm

Surface ladder — four steps, not one

Depth comes from a ladder of surfaces, each one step lighter (dark mode) or shadowed (light mode) than the last: page → card → nested panel → raised control. Borders get two strengths — a whisper for structure, a firmer line for interactive edges. In dark mode, replace drop shadows with a 1px inner top highlight plus a soft ambient shadow; in light mode use small, tight shadows, never big gray blurs.

The display face is the personality budget: page titles, KPI numbers, the login page, and nothing else. Everything functional stays in Geist (already one of your theming engine's options). Mono uppercase micro-labels are what make the sidebar groups, chart captions, and table headers feel engineered rather than default.

Rhythm

A 4px spacing grid (4·8·12·16·24·32·48). Radius scale: 7–8px controls, 11px cards, 999px pills — pick once, apply everywhere; mixed radii are a top "AI-built" tell. Motion: 150ms ease-out on hover states, 250ms on panel transitions, and honor prefers-reduced-motion. No scroll-linked effects — that's implicated in your current jank.

The Setup experience, rebuilt as a two-pane wizard

Today Setup is a long single-column scroll of nine cards — you read all nine to find the one that needs you, and the empty right half of the screen watches you do it. The fix is structural: a stepper rail on the left, one step's detail on the right. The rail shows every state at a glance (done, current, locked-until), the pane holds only the active step, and the width finally works for you. Two rules come with it: the current step is always the first incomplete one, pre-selected on arrival; and steps that depend on others (Call forwarding, Go live) show why they're locked, in plain words, right on the rail.

And one opinionated call: Setup should retire itself. It is a launch surface, not a permanent module. While incomplete, it lives as the progress meter pinned at the bottom of the sidebar (see the dashboard mockup) — clicking it opens this wizard. Once all nine steps are done, the meter is replaced by a one-time "🎉 Live since Sep 4" line and Setup leaves the navigation entirely; a "Launch checklist" entry in Settings keeps it reachable for audits. Permanent nav space for a one-time task is the kind of clutter that makes a product feel like a tool.

Fixing the buried Settings link

You're right that Settings currently sits below the fold of the sidebar — on a laptop it's invisible until you scroll, which makes the product's control panel feel like an afterthought. The fix is a pinned footer cluster, already shown in the dashboard mockup above: the sidebar becomes a flex column where only the middle nav region scrolls (overflow-y:auto on the nav, margin-top:auto on the footer), while Settings and the setup meter stay glued to the bottom at every viewport height. Settings never scrolls out of view again, and it gains an icon and the same hover treatment as every other nav item — pinned, not demoted. Back it up with two redundant paths, because settings is a destination people hunt for: the client switcher's dropdown gets a "Company settings" row, and ⌘K search matches every settings section by name ("branding," "custom fields," "outgoing email") so nobody has to remember where a control lives.

04 · The rules

Ten rules to hold the line

  1. Every number earns context.

    A metric never appears without a comparison — a delta, a sparkline, or a period label. "47" is data; "47 ▲12% this week" is a product.

  2. Four surfaces, two borders, one shadow recipe.

    All depth comes from the ladder. If a new component needs a fifth surface, the design is wrong, not the ladder.

  3. Weight before size, size before color.

    Hierarchy escalates in that order. Reach for the accent color last — it's for actions and identity, not emphasis.

  4. Status is never color alone.

    Booked/Abandoned/Spam pills always carry the dot and the word. Colorblind owners run businesses too.

  5. The whole row is the button.

    Tables: full-row click target, hover surface shift, visible focus ring, and a bulk-action bar that appears the moment a checkbox is ticked.

  6. Empty states sell the feature.

    "No conversations yet" becomes one sentence of what will appear here plus the one action that makes it happen. Blueprints' empty state already does this — make it the template.

  7. Speak the owner's language.

    No milestone codes, no "Released," no {{template_syntax}} in client-facing copy. Read every string as a landscaper at 7 AM.

  8. Motion is a whisper.

    150ms hovers, 250ms panels, reduced-motion respected, nothing tied to scroll. Fix the scroll jank before adding any new animation.

  9. Brand the thresholds.

    Login, booking page, email footers, invite emails — anywhere a client's customer touches the product gets the client's logo and color, automatically, from the tokens you already store.

  10. The dashboard is the invoice.

    Its job is to prove the service paid for itself this week. Design every card on it to answer "what did BIS capture for me?"

06 · The interaction layer

Beyond the redesign: how the app should behave

The tokens and mockups fix how the product looks. What makes users say "this feels expensive" is behavior — the second layer, built on the first.

Speed

⌘K is the spine

One command palette that finds contacts, calls, and conversations, jumps to any settings section by name, and runs actions ("new opportunity," "text Maria"). It solves navigation depth (including the Settings problem) permanently, and power users never touch the sidebar again.

Skeletons, never spinners

Loading states shaped like the content they're waiting for — gray KPI tiles, ghost table rows. A spinner says "wait"; a skeleton says "almost." Combine with optimistic updates: a dragged pipeline card lands instantly and reconciles in the background.

A real keyboard model

j/k to move through rows, Enter to open, Esc to close, ? for the shortcut sheet. Costs a day, and it's the single strongest "built by people who use software" signal to anyone technical evaluating the platform.

Context

Drawers, not page loads

Clicking a call or contact in a list opens a right-side drawer over the list, not a full navigation — triage ten calls without losing your place, Esc to close, deep-link to the full page for heavy editing. This is the modern CRM's core interaction (Linear, Attio, Front all live on it).

Inline edit everywhere

Contact fields, opportunity values, tags: click the value, edit in place, save on blur with an undo toast. Kill the form-and-Save-button pattern everywhere the edit is small.

Design all three states

Every screen ships with its loaded, empty, and error state designed — the empty state selling the feature (rule 06), the error state saying what happened and what to do. Unstyled edge states are where "basic" comes back.

Trust & delight

Sofía has presence

A quiet topbar indicator — "● Sofía · on a call" with a soft pulse, "✓ 47 calls handled this week" when idle. The AI receptionist is the product's hero; right now she's invisible until you open a transcript. Later: click through to a live transcript view.

Undo beats confirm

Reversible actions execute immediately with a 10-second undo toast; confirmation dialogs are reserved for the truly destructive, which ask you to type the thing's name. Fewer interruptions, more safety than a reflexive "Are you sure?".

Español is a toggle, not a translation project

Per-user language switch for the client UI. Half your market thinks in Spanish; a bilingual admin panel is a moat competitors won't copy (pairs with the bilingual receptionist you already have).

Owners live on phones

A responsive PWA pass with a bottom tab bar for the five core sections and push notifications for bookings and hot leads. Not a native app — a home-screen icon and a layout that respects thumbs.

A /styleguide page keeps you honest

One internal route rendering every token, button, pill, card, and state from the live codebase. It's your design system's contract — drift shows up there first, and screenshot tests can watch it.

Notifications with a spine

A bell with a real inbox: new lead, booking, cancellation, review — each row deep-linking to the record, mark-all-read, quiet hours. This is the habit loop that brings owners back daily.
